# Oil-Pastel Style Selection

Use this guide after inspecting the source photo and before writing the finished-art prompt. These are material-and-mark families, not artist imitations. Describe the chosen family through observable properties.

## Style families

### 1. Structural dry-stroke landscape

Best for tree-lined roads, streets, architecture, interiors with strong perspective, and scenes whose rhythm matters more than surface detail.

- Marks: long directional dry strokes plus a few broad side-of-stick masses.
- Edges: broken but structurally aligned; main verticals and vanishing lines stay legible.
- Paper: high exposure, especially in sky, road, walls, and quiet negative space.
- Layering: low to medium; avoid filling every gap.
- Palette: 6–8 restrained earth and sky colors.
- Simplify: reduce twigs, leaves, windows, signs, and ground debris into repeated marks.
- Preserve: perspective, trunk/building rhythm, horizon, major shadows, and the focal silhouette.

### 2. Bold flat-block poster

Best for cluttered photos with a clear silhouette, storefronts, simple objects, graphic travel scenes, and compositions that need stronger readability at small size.

- Marks: wide flat blocks, blunt contour strokes, minimal blending.
- Edges: chunky and decisive, with deliberate shape simplification.
- Paper: medium exposure around shape boundaries.
- Layering: low; color relationships do most of the work.
- Palette: 5–7 colors with one controlled accent.
- Simplify: merge small objects and background planes aggressively.
- Preserve: silhouette, pose, large color relationships, and one secondary anchor.

### 3. Soft atmospheric layering

Best for close portraits, pets, quiet interiors, mist, dusk, snow, and emotionally gentle keepsakes.

- Marks: short overlapping strokes with light pressure and restrained smudging.
- Edges: soft in secondary areas, firmer around identity-critical features.
- Paper: medium-to-low exposure, but texture must remain visible.
- Layering: medium, with two or three transparent-looking color passes rather than smooth gradients.
- Palette: 7–10 close-value colors, usually warm or muted.
- Simplify: soften background objects and micro-texture.
- Preserve: face/character feature lock, expression, pose, and light direction.

### 4. Dense saturated impasto

Best for flowers, food, fruit, colorful still life, close-up plants, and subjects whose appeal is tactile color abundance.

- Marks: short thick overlapping strokes and visible color mixing.
- Edges: irregular and tactile rather than outlined.
- Paper: low-to-medium exposure; leave small breaks to prove the physical surface.
- Layering: high in the focal area, medium elsewhere.
- Palette: 8–12 colors with warm/cool counterpoints.
- Simplify: reduce the number of petals, leaves, reflections, or decorative units.
- Preserve: overall mass, dominant color clusters, and focal highlights.

### 5. Naive contour character

Best for toys, mascots, pets, children, and simple everyday scenes where charm and recognizable character matter more than realistic volume.

- Marks: thick uneven contour plus simple filled color areas.
- Edges: intentionally wobbly but stable across stages.
- Paper: medium exposure.
- Layering: low-to-medium.
- Palette: 6–9 cheerful practical colors.
- Simplify: anatomy, folds, fur, and background into friendly large shapes.
- Preserve: the feature-lock card exactly; naive does not authorize changing eyes, nose, mouth, patches, or accessories.

### 6. Flecked field impression

Best for flower gardens, meadows, foliage, and scenes built from many small color events rather than clean object boundaries.

- Marks: clustered dabs and short curved strokes; change direction by plane instead of scattering marks uniformly.
- Edges: focal blooms or figures receive only a few firmer accents; surrounding masses dissolve into neighboring marks.
- Paper: low-to-medium exposure, with small gaps inside dense fields.
- Layering: medium-to-high locally, but preserve a clear large-value structure underneath.
- Palette: 8–12 colors grouped into two or three dominant families; avoid using every hue everywhere.
- Simplify: represent many flowers, leaves, or grasses as rhythmic clusters, not individually counted objects.
- Preserve: the main sweep of the field, path, horizon, focal cluster, and light-dark grouping.

### 7. Toned-paper selective light

Best for dusk, night, reeds, winter silhouettes, and high-contrast subjects where the paper can act as a middle value.

- Marks: sparse dark structure first, then selective light accents drawn over the toned ground.
- Edges: mostly lost or broken; reserve crisp edges for the brightest focal marks.
- Paper: high exposure. Choose a gray, blue-gray, brown, or other purposeful middle-value paper tone.
- Layering: low overall and concentrated around highlights.
- Palette: 4–7 colors with a strong light-dark hierarchy.
- Simplify: remove most secondary texture and let uncovered paper connect quiet areas.
- Preserve: silhouette rhythm, highlight placement, and the dominant negative-space shape.

### 8. Sculpted angular impasto / 棱角浮雕厚涂

Best for flowers, bold fruit or food, compact still life, and subjects whose volume can be modeled with a limited set of petals or planar strokes.

- Marks: a small number of thick chisel, wedge, or curved strokes; let one stroke describe one petal or form plane. Keep scraped grooves, raised ridges, broken tips, and visible wax deposits.
- Edges: angular, broken, and visibly lifted from the paper; avoid finely feathered, perfectly rounded, or digitally softened edges.
- Paper: low-to-medium exposure around outer contours and quiet spaces so the relief remains selective rather than coating the entire sheet.
- Layering: high and sculptural on the focal subject, thin or flat in the background.
- Palette: 6–10 colors. Allow two partially unmixed colors to coexist inside a thick stroke.
- Lighting and value: use clear side or raking light so bright ridges and dark seams reveal the relief.
- Simplify: collapse many petals, leaves, or food details into a manageable set of large planes and decisive turns.
- Preserve: silhouette, dominant bloom or object masses, focal center, and directional gesture.
- Authenticity: read as matte waxy oil pastel or oil stick with occasional scraper behavior, not wet glossy paint, cake icing, clay, or smooth 3D rendering.

This differs from **Dense saturated impasto**: family 4 builds abundance with many short overlapping strokes; family 8 uses fewer, larger, raised planes with stronger angular edges and relief.

## Selection rules

1. Identify what makes the photo memorable: structure, silhouette, atmosphere, tactile color, or character.
2. Choose the family whose strengths match that memory carrier.
3. Check risk: a small face favors feature protection; dense architecture favors structural dry stroke; weak silhouettes may need flat-block simplification.
4. Write the style card in concrete terms. Do not merely name the family.
5. Use at most one secondary influence. Example: structural dry stroke with a small soft-atmosphere influence to quiet a foggy background.
6. For flowers, choose family 4 when color abundance and overlapping rhythm are the main appeal; choose family 8 when physical relief, large petal planes, and angular raised edges are the main appeal.

## Reference-derived variation axes

When a reference set contains several oil-pastel looks, do not average them into one generic finish. Classify each useful example along these independent axes, then select a coherent combination for the source photo:

- **ground:** white/warm paper, fully covered color field, or exposed toned paper;
- **coverage:** sparse selective marks, balanced broken coverage, or dense near-full coverage;
- **mark grammar:** horizontal rubbed bands, long structural strokes, clustered dabs, curved growth strokes, or chunky decorative ridges;
- **edge hierarchy:** one crisp focal zone with soft surroundings, consistently broken edges, or bold enclosed contours;
- **value design:** airy high-key, middle-value atmospheric, or dark-ground/highlight-led;
- **palette behavior:** restrained analogous, natural warm/cool counterpoint, or deliberately saturated decorative color;
- **detail distribution:** quiet large areas plus one detailed focus, repeated field texture, or evenly descriptive rendering.

For a single output, choose one option on each axis. Do not mix incompatible extremes merely because they appeared in the same reference collection. A real small-format oil-pastel study normally has a dominant mark grammar and a clear limit on where dense detail occurs.

## Small-format realism cues

- Design the image as though it will be drawn at roughly postcard to A4 scale; reduce marks that would be physically smaller than a pastel tip.
- Let pressure and coverage vary in the larger form-building strokes. A consistent fine wax grain or paper tooth may span the whole image and should not be treated as a defect.
- Keep broad calm passages at the structural level, but do not erase fine granular oil-pastel texture merely to make those passages digitally smooth.
- When using a colored ground, treat exposed paper as an intentional color, not as an unfinished error.
- Do not reproduce a reference photo's handwritten size labels, sale marks, signatures, borders, or overlaid text.

## Ambiguity and references

- When two families fit equally well and would produce materially different results, present both briefly and ask the user only if their preference matters.
- When a user supplies visual references, extract recurring stroke width, direction, layering, edge softness, paper exposure, palette, and simplification. Do not copy signatures, watermarks, exact compositions, or artist-specific identifiers.
- If a reference page cannot be inspected, disclose the limitation and do not claim a reference-specific match. The generic family system remains usable, but label the choice as provisional.

## Style acceptance check

Reject or correct the result when any two of these fail:

- stroke scale matches the style card;
- paper exposure matches the family;
- edge behavior is consistent across the image;
- layering density supports rather than buries the focal hierarchy;
- palette stays within the planned range;
- removed detail does not damage identity or structure;
- the result could plausibly be recreated with real oil-pastel sticks.
